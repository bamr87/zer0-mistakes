## The shape of it

Morning on a coastal tower outside Dublin. Three young men share the space:
Stephen, who is renting it; Mulligan, who is louder and funnier and has taken
over; and Haines, an English visitor who is studying the Irish the way one
studies a landscape. They shave, eat, argue pleasantly, and walk down to the
water to swim. Stephen leaves for work and decides, somewhere on the way, that
he is not coming back.

That is the entire plot. Roughly ninety minutes of an ordinary morning.

## The trick

The trick in this episode is **displacement**. Almost nothing anyone says is
about what it is about. Mulligan's jokes are territorial claims. Haines's
curiosity is a form of ownership. The argument about the key to the tower is
not about the key.

The prose plays the same game. It moves between a fairly conventional
third-person narration and Stephen's interior voice without marking the
transition, so a sentence can begin as description and end as grievance. Once
you notice it, you can feel exactly where his attention goes: to the sea, to
his mother, to money, back to the sea.

This is the book teaching you its method on the first pass, in the easiest
possible conditions — few characters, one location, ordinary events. Later it
will do the same thing with none of those supports.

## Threads

**The tower and the key.** Who holds the key, who pays the rent, and who
behaves as though the place is theirs. This is the book's first version of a
question it keeps asking: occupation, tenancy, and who gets to be at home.

**The sea.** It arrives in the first pages and does not leave. Watch what
adjective attaches to it each time; it tracks Stephen's mood with almost
embarrassing directness, and the book knows it.

**Grief, refused.** Stephen's mother has recently died, and he would not do the
thing she asked of him at the end. Mulligan raises it lightly. Stephen does not
respond lightly. Nearly everything unpleasant in his behaviour for the rest of
the day is downstream of this.

**Being watched.** Haines observes; Mulligan performs; Stephen is conscious of
both. The triangle of watcher, performer, and self-conscious third party
repeats across the book with different people in each seat.

## Loose ends

The episode's title never appears in the text, and neither do any of the other
seventeen. The Homeric names are a scaffold Joyce used and then removed. I am
keeping them as labels because they are how everyone refers to the episodes,
but it is worth remembering they are not in the book.

I cannot decide how much sympathy the episode wants for Stephen. He is treated
badly and he is also insufferable, and the prose declines to arbitrate. My
current read is that the refusal is the point — that this is a portrait of
someone whose grief has curdled into a posture, written from close enough to
see the posture. Provisional. Ask me again after the third episode.
