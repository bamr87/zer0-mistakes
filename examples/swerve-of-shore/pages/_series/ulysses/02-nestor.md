## The shape of it

Stephen teaches a history lesson badly, sets the boys a riddle they cannot
solve, stays behind to help a struggling pupil with sums, and then collects his
pay from the headmaster, who lectures him about money, thrift, and the Jews
while getting most of his facts wrong.

Short, contained, and much stranger than it first appears.

## The trick

This episode runs on **mismatched authorities**. Everyone in it is
professionally entitled to explain something and is doing it poorly. Stephen
teaches history he does not believe in. The headmaster dispenses wisdom that is
partly bigotry and partly plain error. Even the riddle Stephen poses has an
answer so arbitrary it functions as a small act of aggression against the
class.

The formal move underneath is that the interior voice has gained confidence.
In the first episode, Stephen's thoughts arrived in short bursts between
dialogue. Here they run longer and stray further, and the prose starts trusting
you to follow an association without explaining it. That trust ratchets up
steadily for the next hundred pages.

## Threads

**History as something done to you.** The episode's famous formulation belongs
to Stephen and is not necessarily the book's position — a distinction worth
holding onto, because the line gets quoted as though Joyce is speaking. He is
not. It is a young man being epigrammatic at his employer.

**Debt.** Stephen is paid, and we learn he owes money widely. The book keeps
very precise accounts of who owes what, and the ledger matters later.

**The struggling pupil.** A brief, genuinely tender scene — Stephen helping a
boy who is bad at sums and sees himself in him. It is the first time he is
unambiguously kind, and it is worth marking, because kindness is not his
default register.

**Anti-semitism, stated plainly.** The headmaster's closing joke establishes
the environment the book's central character is about to walk into. Bloom does
not appear for another episode, and the ground is already prepared.

## Loose ends

The riddle. It has an answer, the answer is deliberately unsatisfying, and I
still cannot tell whether Stephen is being cruel to the boys, cruel to himself,
or performing a bleakness he does not entirely feel. All three readings hold.

I also want to flag how efficient this episode is. Two locations, three
conversations, maybe forty minutes of story time, and it installs the money
ledger, the history problem, and the book's central prejudice. If you are
finding the early going slow, this is the one to reread first — the density is
easiest to see at this length.
