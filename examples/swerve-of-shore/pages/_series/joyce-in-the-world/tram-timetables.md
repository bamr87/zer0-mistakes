## Why the geography matters

*Ulysses* is set on one day in one city, and the city is not decoration. Routes are walkable in the time the book allots them. Characters who pass each other are passing each other at plausible places. When two people turn up in the same street, the question of whether that is chance or design is a real question, because the book has built a system in which either is possible.

This is unusual. Most novels handle distance loosely — a character arrives when the scene needs them. Here, the scaffolding is load-bearing, and reading with a map turns a lot of apparent coincidence into something more interesting: a demonstration that a city of that size produces near-misses at a steady rate without anyone arranging them.

## What to actually use

**A period street map.** Any reproduction of a 1900s Dublin map will do. The street layout is largely intact, so a modern map works for most of the walking — but the tram network is gone, and the trams matter.

**A chapter-by-chapter time chart.** Several exist. The episodes are pinned to particular hours, and the overlaps are the point: while one thread is in a newspaper office, another is walking past the door.

**Your own notes on who is where.** This is the one I would recommend building yourself rather than importing. Keeping a rough page-margin record of location and time as you read is more useful than consulting a finished chart, because it forces you to notice the transitions.

## What it changes

Three things, in my reading.

**Near-misses become structural.** Once you can see the routes, the number of almost-meetings stops reading as authorial coyness and starts reading as an argument about how cities work.

**The middle of the book gets easier.** There is an episode built entirely from short simultaneous fragments scattered across the city. Without geography it is disorienting by design. With it, it is a satisfying puzzle — the fragments snap to locations and the whole thing acquires a shape.

**Distance becomes emotional.** Some of the book's most affecting turns are about how far one character has walked to avoid going home. That does not register unless you know the distance.

## A caution

It is possible to over-invest here. The map is an aid, not a key — there is a kind of reading that becomes municipal trainspotting and loses the book entirely. The rule I use: consult the map when the text made you feel lost, not whenever a street name appears.
