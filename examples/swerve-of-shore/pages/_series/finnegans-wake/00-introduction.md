Every account of reading *Finnegans Wake* starts with an apology, so here is mine: I do not understand this book, I will not understand it by the end of this series, and I am going to write about it anyway.

That is not false modesty. It is the only workable posture. The Wake is constructed so that comprehension in the ordinary sense is not available — the words are portmanteaus running several languages at once, the narrative is cyclical, and the book ends mid-sentence in a way that loops back to its own opening. A reader waiting to understand it before writing about it waits forever.

## What I am doing instead

**Reading for sound first.** Read aloud. Almost everyone who reports getting somewhere with this book reports the same thing: it is far more tractable as noise than as text. Meaning arrives late and sideways, after rhythm.

**Reading in company.** The Wake is the most social difficult book there is — reading groups have been working through it for decades, some of them slower than a page a month. Alone, it is a wall. Read out loud with two other people, it becomes something closer to a puzzle night.

**Small units, low expectations.** A page is a reasonable session. Two is ambitious. The book does not reward speed and it does not punish stopping.

**Writing down what surfaced.** Not what a passage means — what came up while reading it. A word you half-recognised, a rhythm that turned into a song, a joke you got three seconds late. That is the real record of reading this book, and it is the only one I can honestly keep.

## The shape of the thing

Four books, wildly unequal. The first is the longest and the one most people get furthest into; it introduces the family that the whole book keeps re-staging in different costumes — a father, a mother, twin sons, a daughter — and a rumour about something the father may or may not have done. That rumour never resolves. It is not supposed to.

The river runs through all of it. The book's most legible pleasure is hydrological: water, tributaries, tides, the sound of a river being talked about by two people at dusk. If you read nothing else, read the passage people send each other about the washerwomen on the riverbank, aloud, and see whether it does the thing.

## What I will post

An entry per session, following the same headings as the *Ulysses* series where they apply, with the loose ends section doing much heavier lifting. Expect the entries to be shorter, less confident, and more frequently revised.

If you have read further than I have — which is likely — corrections are welcome and get folded in with a credit.
