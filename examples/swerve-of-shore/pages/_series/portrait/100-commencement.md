## The shape of it

*A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man* moves through childhood, school, religious crisis, and a late decision: Stephen refuses the life arranged for him and leaves the country to become an artist. The final pages shift into diary entries — the narration hands the book over to him — and he departs on a note of enormous, uncomplicated ambition.

It is one of the great endings, and it is completely undercut by the book that follows it.

## The trick

The formal argument of *Portrait* is that **style tracks consciousness**. The prose begins in the vocabulary of a small child and grows up in step with its subject — sentence length, abstraction, and self-regard all rising together. By the closing section the language is capable of the exact grandiosity Stephen has grown into, and it delivers that grandiosity without visible irony.

Whether the irony is there anyway is the central critical fight about this novel, and I think the answer is that the book withholds judgment on purpose so that the reader has to supply it. The ending is sincere *and* it is a young man's ending. Both, at once, with no narrator stepping in to mark the difference.

## Threads

**Flight.** Wings, escape, the boy named for a mythical craftsman whose son flew too high. The novel is unembarrassed about this imagery in a way the later book never is.

**Refusal.** Stephen's defining gesture is declining — a vocation, a country, a request from his mother. He is not defined by what he makes, because he has not made anything. He is defined by what he will not do.

**The family in decline.** The household gets steadily poorer across the novel, in the background, mostly unremarked. It is the quiet engine under a lot of the plot.

## Loose ends

Here is the thing that keeps me coming back. *Ulysses* opens with Stephen having returned — the departure did not take, his mother died, he came home, and he is now renting a tower he cannot afford and teaching school badly. The later book does not comment on this. It does not need to.

Read in sequence, the great ending of *Portrait* becomes something else: not a triumph but a stated intention that the world declined to honour. And the strange effect is that this makes *Portrait* better rather than smaller. An ending that means what it says is worth less than one that means what it says and is also wrong.

Which is why I would rather read them in order, and why this series exists alongside the main sequence instead of before it.
